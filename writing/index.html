4-Grid Picture Writing

Find the problem first, then expand outward. Use a hidden gem (伏筆) to connect the story, use dialogue and descriptive phrases to move the plot on, and use contrast to show what the character has learnt.

Colour key: problem hidden gem / 伏筆 dialogue descriptive phrase beginning ending

1 Look at the 4 pictures

One picture, one paragraph. You need at least 4 pictures and 4 paragraphs. Write what happens in that picture. Do not squeeze four pictures into two paragraphs, and do not skip a picture.

Picture 1→Paragraph 1 · Picture 2→Paragraph 2 · Picture 3→Paragraph 3 · Picture 4→Paragraph 4
Hidden traps & tricks

Paragraphing

Trap
✗ Do not write Pictures 1–4 as one long paragraph.
✓ One picture, one paragraph.
Trap
✗ Do not skip Picture 3 and jump to the ending.
✓ Something must happen in every picture, so the ending feels natural.

2 Find the problem first, then expand outward

Do not start by looking only at Picture 1 and asking what happens at the beginning. First look at all four pictures and ask: Which picture shows the real problem? It is often in the middle (usually Picture 2). After you find the problem, expand outward:

1. Where is the problem?→ 2. What happened before?→ 3. How is it solved?→ 4. What has changed?
Ask first
Which picture shows the trouble? Which one is the “something went wrong” picture?
Then expand
Before: Why did this happen? After: How is it solved? What has the character learnt?
Why this order?
If you start from the problem, the story has a clear direction. You will not reach Picture 3 and suddenly realise you do not know how to end it.

3 Keep the story flowing

The story should progress naturally. Each paragraph continues from the previous picture. Do not stop suddenly, and do not add a new ending that was never prepared.

Good flow
He ignored the alarm clock. The next morning, he woke up late… That evening, he set the clock beside his bed. The following day, he arrived early.
Jumping / sudden ending
✗ He was late. Then he was a good boy. The end.
There is no solving process, so readers cannot see how he learnt to be punctual.

Useful links: The next morning · Suddenly · At that moment · After that · The following day. Use them to move time on, not just a pile of and then.

4 Locate the hidden gem (伏筆)

A hidden gem / 伏筆 is a small object in the pictures that is related to the problem. You can find it in the pictures, or you can reasonably add it. Use it so the problem appears naturally, and so the solution has a root.

How to find it: lock onto the problem first, then ask, “Which small object could cause or solve this problem?” Then go back to an earlier picture and circle that object — it is often already there, but students have not noticed it.

Example page A · The alarm clock

Find the problem first: In Picture 2 he is late for school.

Then ask: Why is he late? Is there a small object in the pictures that is related to time?

Circle the hidden gem: the unused twin-bell alarm clock on the desk. It is already in Picture 1, but it has not been set.

How it connects the story: The problem (being late) comes from ignoring the clock. The solution (being on time) comes from putting the clock beside the bed and setting it. The same object runs through all four pictures.

Example page B · The extra lunchbox

Find the problem first: A classmate has forgotten her lunch and is hungry.

Then ask: Is there a small object in the main character’s bag that could help?

Circle the hidden gem: the extra lunchbox in the school bag (Mum packed one more).

How it connects the story: At first she wants to hide it. Later she feels it in her bag and decides to share it. The same lunchbox makes the change from selfish to generous feel natural, not sudden.

Example page C · The star keychain

Find the problem first: After school he cannot find his house key and cannot get in.

Then ask: Where was the key? Is there a small object that has been hanging on the bag all along?

Circle the hidden gem: the star keychain swinging from the zipper. In Picture 1 it is already hanging loosely.

How it connects the story: Because it is loose, it can fall off. Because a classmate finds the keychain, the problem can be solved. He learns to clip the keychain inside his bag.

Hidden traps & tricks

The hidden gem is not decoration

Weak
✗ Only write “There was a pretty keychain.” and never mention it again.
Strong
✓ The hidden gem should appear early, help cause or explain the problem, and be used again in the solution.
Everyday objects
You can use a water bottle, a ticket, a cue card, or another everyday object. The important point is that it is related to the problem, not that it looks pretty.

6 Meaningful dialogue

Do not use dialogue only to say hello. A useful line should do at least one of these things: move the problem or the solution forward, and let readers hear how the character feels.

Weak · empty greeting
✗ “Hi.” “Hello.” “How are you?” “I’m fine.”
The story does not move forward.
Strong · move the problem on
✓ “Where is my key? I can’t get in!”
Readers know the problem at once, and they can hear that he is worried.
Strong · move the solution on
✓ “Mum packed an extra one. You can have it.”
The dialogue itself is the solution.
Strong · show feeling
✓ “What if I forget everything?” he whispered.
Words like whispered / cried / groaned / promised help you show feelings.
Hidden traps & tricks

Punctuation and speaking verbs

✓ “I’m early today!” Ken smiled.

✓ “I can’t start,” Hugo stammered.

Do not use said in every sentence. Try: whispered, cried, muttered, groaned, promised, called, stammered. Match the verb to the feeling. Do not pick one at random.

7 Descriptive phrases

Do not depend on dialogue alone. Descriptive phrases can show time, actions, objects and feelings, so the next picture follows naturally.

What can we see?+ How does it feel?+ Where is the hidden gem?
Weak
✗ He was late. He was sad. Then he was good.
Strong · an object moves the story on
✓ The twin-bell alarm clock sat quietly on the desk, but he did not touch it.
Strong · a feeling moves the story on
✓ His face turned red and his hands would not stop shaking.
Strong · a time link
✓ The school yard was still quiet when he walked in, calm and ready.

8 Useful vocabulary

Use these words to write what happens and how people feel. You may reuse them in different stories, but the sentences must fit your own four pictures.

Character change
late → punctual
selfish → generous
careless → careful / responsible
impatient → patient
embarrassed / shy → brave
messy → responsible
Feelings
worried, embarrassed, restless, proud, relieved, sorry, determined, grateful
Story verbs
rushed, dangled, squeezed, stammered, fastened, offered, stepped back, ticked
Time / flow
that evening, the next morning, at that moment, after a deep breath, from now on, the following day
Hidden-gem nouns
alarm clock, lunchbox, keychain, queue ticket, cue card, chore chart, water bottle, notepad

9 Sample writing (Set 1)

These four paragraphs use all the skills above: one picture, one paragraph; start from the problem; a hidden gem that runs through the story; meaningful dialogue; descriptive phrases that move the story on; and a clear contrast between the beginning and the ending. The colours match the key at the top.

Paragraph 1 · Picture 1
After dinner, Ken sat at the piano and kept practising. “Just one more song. I won’t be late tomorrow,” he whispered. The twin-bell alarm clock sat quietly on the desk, but he did not touch it. He was usually late, and tonight he did not change that habit.

Paragraph 2 · Picture 2 · problem
The next morning, Ken opened his eyes and jumped up. Daylight was already bright. He grabbed his bag and ran. “Oh no! The gate is closing!” he cried. He rushed down the street with his bag half-zipped, his heart beating fast. He was late for school again.

Paragraph 3 · Picture 3
That evening, Ken looked at the alarm clock and thought about the morning. “I should set the alarm beside my bed tonight,” he said firmly. He placed it next to his pillow and checked the time twice. This time, the little clock would really help him.

Paragraph 4 · Picture 4
The following day, the alarm rang and Ken got up at once. The school yard was still quiet when he walked in, calm and ready. “Good morning, Miss Chan. I’m early today!” he smiled. Ken was no longer the boy who was always late. He had learnt to be punctual.

When you write another set, use the same frame and change the hidden gem and the contrast — for example, the extra lunchbox: selfish → generous.
